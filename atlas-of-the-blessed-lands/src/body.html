    Notes
    Layers

    Layers

    No boundary for any of these lands is set by revelation.

    The Qur'an names blessed places but never uses the words al-Shām, Filasṭīn or al-Ḥijāz. The Sunnah and the Companions use al-Shām and al-Ḥijāz as regions everyone already knew. The lines came later: from ʿUmar's military districts (ajnād), from jurists defining al-Ḥijāz for a single ruling, and from geographers who disagree at every edge.

    Only two places have a boundary the texts actually fix: the ḥarams of Makkah and Madinah, shown in detail below the map. On the map, solid colour marks land that every major source includes and hatching marks land that only some include.

    Reading the map

    Lines and colour

    Solid: well established. Coasts and present-day borders.
    Dashed: approximate. The landmarks are known but the line between them is not, such as the ḥaram outlines and the old roads.
    Dotted: disputed or uncertain. The sources disagree, the edge moved over time, or I found no source for a line and drew only a rough indication.
    Solid colour: land that every major source includes in the region.
    Hatching: land that some sources include and others leave out. Where two regions' hatching crosses, the sources disagree about which region the land belongs to.

    The shaded relief shows mountains and escarpments; the sand stipple marks the great deserts; the faint grid is latitude and longitude.

    Four layers of evidence

    Each layer is kept separate. Where I connect them, it is marked as my synthesis. A dagger † marks a statement I took from the atlas you shared and did not re-check against the sources.

    Tier 1

    Revelation

    The Qur'an and the authentic Sunnah. Boundaries set: none, except the two ḥarams.

    al-Shām

    • The word does not occur in the Qur'an. Its root appears only as al-mashʾamah, “the left” (56:9, 90:19).
    • “The land We blessed” (21:71, 21:81, 7:137, 34:18). Al-Baghawī explains 21:71 and 21:81 as al-Shām.
    • The Sunnah uses al-Shām as a known region: al-Juḥfah is the mīqāt for it (al-Bukhārī 1524), and a fire from the land of al-Ḥijāz will light the necks of the camels at Buṣrā (al-Bukhārī 7118). I found no authenticated hadith that defines its borders.

    Filasṭīn

    • The name does not occur in the Qur'an.
    • 17:1: al-Aqṣā, “whose surroundings We have blessed”: a blessing, not a boundary.
    • 5:21, “the holy land”: five answers in the early commentary (row 9).

    al-Ḥijāz

    • The word does not occur. Its root appears only as ḥājiz, “a barrier” (27:61, 69:47).
    • The Sunnah names “the land of al-Ḥijāz” (al-Bukhārī 7118) and commands the expulsion of the mushrikūn from Jazīrat al-ʿArab (al-Bukhārī 3053, Muslim 1637), a ruling that gave jurists a reason to define al-Ḥijāz.

    Neighbouring lands

    • Miṣr is named (12:99; also 10:87, 12:21, 43:51). In 2:61 the word is miṣran, which al-Ṭabarī reports Qatādah, al-Suddī, Mujāhid and Ibn Zayd reading as "any town", and he leaves the question open. Sabaʾ is named (34:15–19), and the Prophet ﷺ said Sabaʾ was a man with ten sons (al-Tirmidhī 3222). al-Aḥqāf (46:21) and al-Rūm (30:2–4) are named.
    • al-Ḥabashah is not in the Qur'an; it is in the Sunnah.
    • I found no verse or authentic hadith that calls Iraq a ḥaram, holy or blessed. The Sunnah gives it a mīqāt.
    Tier 2

    Companions and early scholars

    1st–3rd c. AH. Working divisions, not a fixed map.

    al-Shām

    • ʿUmar organised it into ajnād: Filasṭīn, al-Urdunn, Dimashq and Ḥimṣ. Qinnasrīn was split off later, under Muʿāwiyah or Yazīd, and al-ʿAwāṣim under Hārūn al-Rashīd.
    • At Sargh ʿUmar was met by “the commanders of the ajnād” (al-Bukhārī, Muslim), so the districts existed in the Companions' time.
    • Al-Balādhurī: ʿUmar counted Taymāʾ and Wādī al-Qurā as al-Shām (badge E). Al-Ṭabarī: all agree the Holy Land lies between the Euphrates and al-ʿArīsh (badge A).

    Filasṭīn

    • Early usage treats Filasṭīn as a named part inside al-Shām: Ibrāhīm settled at al-Sabʿ “of the land of Filasṭīn” (Ibn Isḥāq, badge M); Usāmah was sent to al-Dārūm (badge N).
    • On 30:3, Muqātil names “al-Urdunn and Filasṭīn” side by side (badge O).
    • Jund Filasṭīn's capital was Ludd, then al-Ramlah, founded around 715 CE.

    al-Ḥijāz

    • Physical sense: the mountain chain that “bars” low Tihāmah from high Najd (Hishām b. al-Kalbī); al-Aṣmaʿī: Makkah is Tihāmī, Madinah and al-Ṭāʾif are Ḥijāzī.
    • Legal sense: al-Shāfiʿī, Makkah, Madinah, al-Yamāmah and their districts; Aḥmad adds Khaybar, Yanbuʿ and Fadak † (badge J).
    • Outlier: al-Ḥarbī, “Tabūk and Filasṭīn are of al-Ḥijāz” (via Yāqūt). Not drawn.

    Neighbouring lands

    • On 30:3 Mujāhid takes “the nearest land” to be al-Jazīrah. On 46:21 three early reports place al-Aḥqāf at al-Shiḥr (Qatādah), Mahrah (Muqātil) or between ʿUmān and Mahrah (Ibn ʿAbbās).
    Tier 3

    Classical geographers

    3rd–8th c. AH. Explicit lines, but they differ.

    al-Shām

    • Ibn Ḥawqal (4th c.): the Sea of al-Rūm to the west; the desert “from Ayla to the Euphrates” to the east; the Euphrates up to the Byzantine frontier, taking in the frontier fortresses; Egypt to the south, ending at Rafaḥ.
    • Yāqūt (d. 626 AH): length from the Euphrates to al-ʿArīsh; width from the two mountains of Ṭayyiʾ to the sea (badges A, F). The widest line.
    • Abū al-Fidāʾ (d. 732 AH): a narrower settled-land line, roughly the solid core.
    • Al-Muqaddasī counts six districts, adding al-Sharāt †.

    Filasṭīn

    • The geographers list Filasṭīn as a district of al-Shām, the westernmost, from Rafaḥ to al-Lajjūn (badge K).
    • Disputed towns include Arīḥāʾ and ʿAmmān (badge L).
    • Nāṣir-i Khusraw notes that al-Ramlah itself was called “Filasṭīn” †. Under the Mamluks and Ottomans the unit broke into smaller districts, though the name stayed in use †.

    al-Ḥijāz

    • Yāqūt gathers the earlier definitions and records Sargh as “the first of al-Ḥijāz” (badge D).
    • Al-Qazwīnī: “a barrier between Yemen and al-Shām, a month's journey” †.
    • Result: a firm core (Makkah, al-Ṭāʾif, Madinah, Yanbuʿ, Khaybar, Fadak) with a soft north, south and east.

    Neighbouring lands

    • Al-Iṣṭakhrī gives al-Jazīrah, al-ʿIrāq and Miṣr chapters of their own. Yāqūt on Miṣr: from the two trees between Rafaḥ and al-ʿArīsh to Aswān, and from Barqah to Aylah.
    • Yāqūt on Tihāmah: it runs along the sea, and Makkah is of it.
    Synthesis (mine)The seam between al-Shām and al-Ḥijāz is a band, not a line. ʿUmar, per al-Balādhurī, put it at Wādī al-Qurā; the geographers' “first of al-Ḥijāz, last of al-Shām” sits at Sargh, north of Tabūk. The two are a few hundred kilometres apart, and both are defensible readings of their own time. The map shows them overlapping rather than choosing.
    Tier 4

    Modern political borders

    20th century to today. Surveyed lines, drawn for other reasons.

    al-Shām

    • After 1918 the British and French mandates divided it into Syria, Lebanon, Palestine and Transjordan; Hatay joined Türkiye in 1939 †.
    • None of these borders follows an old jund line.

    Filasṭīn

    • The Mandate border is not Jund Filasṭīn. It added Galilee, Acre, Haifa, Tiberias and Baysān, all old al-Urdunn, and the Negev to Eilat; it left out the east-bank lands that some sources gave Filasṭīn †.

    al-Ḥijāz

    • Now mainly Saudi Arabia's Makkah and Madinah provinces and part of the Tabūk region.
    • Maʿān and ʿAqaba belonged to the Hashemite Kingdom of the Ḥijāz until 1925, when Britain attached them to Transjordan †.

    Neighbouring lands

    • Al-Jazīrah is split between Syria, Türkiye and Iraq; al-Ḥabashah lies in Ethiopia and Eritrea; Tihāmah is split between Saudi Arabia and Yemen.

    The map uses Natural Earth's de facto lines. They are not an endorsement of any claim; see the honest limits below.

    Four different words

    Ḥaramحَرَم
    A sanctuary in which Allah ﷻ forbade hunting and cutting the vegetation. Makkah is a ḥaram by the agreement of the Muslims, and Madinah by the view of most scholars (the Ḥanafī school is reported to differ; not re-checked). Whether Wajj near al-Ṭāʾif is a third is disputed, and its hadith is graded weak by al-Albānī.
    Muqaddasمُقَدَّس
    Holy or purified. The Qur'an uses it for al-arḍ al-muqaddasah (al-Māʾidah 5:21) and the valley of Ṭuwā (Ṭā Hā 20:12; al-Nāziʿāt 79:16), where al-Baghawī glosses it “purified”. The same Arabic is rendered “blessed valley” in 20:12 and “sacred valley” in 79:16 by Saheeh International. The map does not treat these lands as a ḥaram; whether special rulings attach to them is a question for the scholars.
    Mubārakمُبَارَك
    Blessed. Used for the House at Bakkah (3:96), the surroundings of al-Aqṣā (17:1), the land of 7:137, 21:71 and 21:81, the villages of 34:18, and the spot where Mūsā عليه السلام was called (28:30); in the Sunnah, for Wādī al-ʿAqīq (al-Bukhārī 1534). Al-Baghawī explains the blessing in several of these as water, trees, fruit and abundance.
    Faḍīlaفَضِيلَة
    A stated virtue, “best”. The Prophet ﷺ said of Makkah that it is the best of the land of Allah ﷻ (al-Tirmidhī 3925, Ibn Mājah 3108; ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ gharīb), and the Sunnah gives virtues to Madinah, Qubāʾ, Uḥud and others. Scholars are reported to differ on whether Makkah or Madinah is better; I did not re-check each school's position.

    What is firm, what is approximate, what is disputed

    Firm

    • That Makkah and Madinah are each a ḥaram.
    • The landmarks of Madinah's ḥaram: ʿAyr, Thawr and the two lava fields, named in authentic hadith.
    • The four mīqāts fixed by the Prophet ﷺ, and Dhāt ʿIrq, set by ʿUmar.
    • The location of al-Masjid al-Aqṣā in Jerusalem.
    • That the “blessed land” of several verses is al-Shām or part of it (and, for 7:137, Miṣr as well), as al-Baghawī explains.
    • That Miṣr, Sabaʾ, al-Aḥqāf and al-Rūm are named in the Qur'an.
    • The seaward sides of al-Shām and Jazīrat al-ʿArab.

    Approximate

    • The exact outline of each ḥaram between its markers.
    • The precise spots of the old mīqāt sites and of the Hajj-road stations.
    • The course of the caravan and Hajj roads between stations, and of the Maʾrib road.
    • Al-Shām's frontier near al-ʿArīsh and its edge along the Euphrates.
    • The inland edge of al-Ḥijāz toward Najd.
    • The Nile ribbon and Delta (traced from the river's course), and the places of Dābiq, al-Aʿmāq, Maʾrib, Aksūm and Jawāthā.

    Disputed or uncertain

    • How far al-arḍ al-muqaddasah extends: the early commentators give five answers.
    • Al-Shām's desert edge and southern edge; Yāqūt's description reaches as far as the mountains of Ṭayyiʾ.
    • The borders between the ajnād, which shifted over time.
    • The northern limit of al-Ḥijāz and of Jazīrat al-ʿArab.
    • The locations of Ṭuwā, Mount Sinai and Madyan.
    • Whether the residence ruling covers al-Ḥijāz only or all of Jazīrat al-ʿArab.
    • Every limit I drew dotted: al-ʿIrāq, the north and south ends of al-Jazīrah, Najd, al-Baḥrayn, Tihāmah's north end and al-Rūm's east edge. I found no source for a line.

    Reading these maps honestly

    The lines are my reconstruction

    Classical authors describe limits by naming towns, deserts and rivers. I placed the lines from those descriptions, from the real courses of rivers and coasts, and from modern studies. Treat every edge as rough, tens of kilometres at best, and the dotted edges as ranges, not lines.

    The districts moved over time

    The ajnād of 640 CE, 900 CE and 1050 CE were not identical; the borders drawn here are schematic.

    Discs mark places, not areas

    A numbered disc stands for a place or a whole land, not for its extent. Some discs are moved off their site, with a thin line leading back, so the labels stay readable. Where the sources give no edge (al-Aqṣā's surroundings, the Holy Land, “the land We blessed”), the map uses a dashed or dotted ring, never a line.

    Even the two ḥaram lines are drawn by hand

    In the Makkah detail map the points are the boundary sites al-Nawawī names, placed at approximate positions, with the curve between them interpolated. Madinah's outline joins the landmarks the hadith names and is looser still. The Sinai marker is the traditional Jabal Mūsā, not an identification the Qur'an makes. The enclosure of al-Aqṣā is a small hand-drawn shape.

    The new neighbouring lands

    The river-bounded shapes (al-Jazīrah, the Sawād, the Nile valley and Delta) follow real river courses; the edges that rest on towns or distances, with no source I could read, are dotted. Najd, al-Baḥrayn and Tihāmah are rough indications only. The sizes of the three al-Aḥqāf rings are illustrative.

    Modern borders come from Natural Earth

    They are public-domain de facto lines. They do not show recent changes on the ground, and several are disputed: Jerusalem, the West Bank and Gaza, the Golan Heights, Shebaa and Ḥalāʾib are drawn as hatched disputed areas. The atlas you shared makes claims about which states recognise the Golan annexation and about the buffer zone since December 2024; I could not check them, so I left them out.

    What I could not verify

    • The geographers' formulas for the limits of al-ʿIrāq (Tikrīt to ʿAbbādān and the like), al-Jazīrah, Najd, al-Yamāmah and al-Baḥrayn. I tried again and the pages I could reach gave summaries, not the original text, so I added no limit I could not read myself.
    • The Ḥijāz lists attributed to al-Shāfiʿī and Aḥmad, and the Ḥanafī position on Madinah's ḥaram.
    • The shape of al-Ḥabashah beyond Aksūm.
    • The Golan recognition claims.

    What I changed from the atlas you shared

    • Dropped the 12-mīl ḥimā circle around Madinah: Muslim 1372, as I read it, does not state twelve mīl.
    • Dropped “al-Juḥfah for Egypt”: I could not confirm the narration, so al-Juḥfah is described only as the mīqāt for al-Shām.
    • Kept Ṭuwā's two renderings apart (“blessed valley”, 20:12; “sacred valley”, 79:16) and marked Dhū Ṭuwā near Makkah as a different place.
    • Gave Wajj's hadith its grade (ḍaʿīf, al-Albānī), and did not take the atlas's treatment of Jericho and Nābulus as settled: the notes say the sources disagree about those towns.

    A caliphate outline a thought experiment · not a ruling

    Two outlines, kept apart. The first is mine: the smallest single territory holding every land the sources give a status. The second is yours: the wider area you specified. No text, early scholar or geographer drew either one. The texts on the caliphate are in the first column.

    From revelation

    What the texts say

    Qur'an 24:55. لَيَسْتَخْلِفَنَّهُمْ فِى ٱلْأَرْضِ "Allāh has promised those who have believed among you and done righteous deeds that He will surely grant them succession [to authority] upon the earth just as He granted it to those before them…" (Saheeh International). Al-Baghawī reports from Abū al-ʿĀliyah that it came down when the Companions lived in fear, and says the verse points to the caliphate of Abū Bakr and the imamate of the Rightly Guided Caliphs. Ibn Kathīr describes the promise as fulfilled in the Prophet's ﷺ lifetime and under Abū Bakr, ʿUmar and ʿUthmān.

    Thirty years, then kingship. "Al-Khilafah will be in my Ummah for thirty years, then there will be monarchy after that" (al-Tirmidhī 2226, from Safīnah; graded ḥasan).

    A return foretold. A hadith through al-Nuʿmān b. Bashīr lists prophethood, then a caliphate on the method of prophethood, then biting kingship, then tyrannical kingship, "then there will be a caliphate on the method of prophethood", after which the Prophet ﷺ fell silent (my rendering of the Arabic). Al-Albānī graded its chain ḥasan.

    A warning sign. "Ibn Hawalah, when you see the caliphate has settled in the holy land, earthquakes, sorrows and serious matters will have drawn near" (Abū Dāwūd 2535; ṣaḥīḥ according to al-Albānī).

    None of these texts gives the caliphate a border.

    My synthesis

    The best-lands outline

    It is the smallest single territory that holds every land the sources give a status: al-Ḥijāz with the two ḥarams (entries 1–7), al-Shām with Filasṭīn and al-Aqṣā (8–11), Sinai and al-Ṭūr (12), al-Yaman (14) and Miṣr (15). Where the sources disagree on an edge, I took the widest reading, so every hatched zone of al-Shām and al-Ḥijāz is inside. For Miṣr I took only the Nile valley and the Delta, not Yāqūt's desert rectangle. Tihāmah joins al-Ḥijāz to al-Yaman.

    What falls outside is telling. Najd, al-ʿIrāq, the Gulf coast, al-Ḥabashah and Bilād al-Rūm have no established status (entries 16, 20 and 21), so they are not in it. The table still shows a tenth of Iraq: its western desert, which falls inside only because al-Shām's widest reading reaches the Euphrates. The best lands form a ring around the Red Sea and up the Levant.

Last, to join those lands up, it takes in, at your request: the rest of Italy and all the rest of France, with Corsica and Andorra; Austria, Slovenia and the rest of Croatia and Hungary, which join Italy to the Balkans; and the rest of Ukraine with the Southern and North Caucasus districts of Russia, which join the Black Sea lands to the Caucasus, the Caspian and Kazakhstan. No caliphate in the counted list is known to have ruled the western Mediterranean ones; I did not research the Balkan and Black Sea ones again, so some may have had an Ottoman or Crimean overlord at some time, which I have not checked. The page marks all of them as your addition, and the rule behind them is mine, not the sources’: whole countries or districts, only as many as it takes for every land to touch the next by land. The name Greater Caliphate is yours; the edges of the historical lands are my approximate tracing.

    How I read the two "Greater" terms. Greater Syria: Syria, Lebanon, Jordan, Israel and Palestine, plus Bilād al-Shām's belt in Türkiye. "Greater Israel" is a term from Zionist maximalism and its critics, not an Islamic category; its widest reading runs from the Nile to the Euphrates. With all of Iraq and Egypt already in, neither term adds land of its own.

    How I read "Greater Iraq". You chose both readings: Khuzestan (al-Ahwāz), the modern irredentist claim, and ʿIrāq al-ʿAjam, the "Persian Iraq" of the later geographers, which is the old Jibāl of western Iran (Hamadān, Iṣfahān, Rayy, Qum, Qazvīn, Kirmānshāh). In the complete version, with the whole of Iran inside, they add no land of their own; their names stay on the map to show where they lie. The version to Iraq’s border leaves both out.

    What the sources say about the added lands. The atlas finds no established sacred status for most of them, Iraq included (entries 16, 20 and 21). Cyprus has none either; Ibn Kathīr, on 24:55, lists it among the lands taken in ʿUthmān's time. Türkiye, with Istanbul on both sides of the Bosphorus, is roughly the old Bilād al-Rūm. The hadith promise forgiveness to the first army to campaign against Caesar's city and count the conquest of Constantinople among the events before the Hour: a virtue for an army, not a status for the land (entry 21). Iran is the old land of Fāris and the Jibāl: Ibn Kathīr, on 24:55, describes most of Persia taken under ʿUmar, and Khurāsān and al-Ahwāz under ʿUthmān. That is conquest history, not a status. Tunisia, roughly the old Ifrīqiya, has none either: Ibn Kathīr, on 24:55, names Kairouan among the lands of the west conquered under ʿUthmān (the name Ifrīqiya for Tunisia is from general knowledge). The South Caucasus, the old Armenia and Arrān, has no status in the sources either, and most people in Georgia and Armenia are Christian. Turkmenistan, Afghanistan and Pakistan, roughly the old Khurāsān with part of Khwārazm, and al-Sind with its borderlands, have no status in the sources either: Ibn Kathīr, on 24:55, names Khurāsān among the lands conquered under ʿUthmān, and modern historians date the conquest of Sind to the Umayyads, around 711–714 (the region names and the Sind date are from general knowledge). The Ottoman lands in Europe and in Algeria have no status in the sources either: they entered Islamic history through Ottoman conquest between the 14th and 17th centuries, and this atlas counts the Ottomans as a caliphate only from 1517 (dates from general knowledge). For al-Ḥabashah the Sunnah says the opposite of a claim: دَعُوا الْحَبَشَةَ مَا وَدَعُوكُمْ "Let the Abyssinians alone as long as they let you alone" (Abū Dāwūd 4302; ḥasan according to al-Albānī).

    Precedent and limits

    What it would mean

    It has existed before. Ibn Kathīr, on 24:55, describes Abū Bakr taking control of the whole Arabian Peninsula, and ʿUmar's reign adding the rest of al-Shām, Egypt and most of Persia. By the end of ʿUmar's caliphate, most of the lands in the best-lands outline were under one rule, inside a much larger state. This part is my own summary of general history, not checked against a source in this atlas: the outline uses the widest reading of al-Shām, and its frontier fortresses (Ṭarsūs, Marʿash, Malaṭyah) were probably not yet securely held. Much of your wider outline, including al-Ḥabashah, was not under that rule either.

    Today the best-lands outline would cross ten states and territories, and yours twenty-five, among them Israel, the state that serves the two ḥarams, and countries where most people are not Muslim. The only road to such a union without war is the free consent of the people who live there. In 2014, 122 scholars wrote to the leader of ISIS, who had declared a caliphate by force: "It is forbidden in Islam to declare a caliphate without consensus from all Muslims."

    Muslims disagree about the caliphate today. Some hold that restoring it is an obligation. Others hold that its aims, such as justice and the protection of religion and life, can be served by cooperating states. Many read the hadith of a returning caliphate as news of what Allah will bring about, not as a plan for any group to carry out. This page does not settle that question; ask qualified scholars.

    My reading, not a ruling: the texts describe what will happen and tie a caliphate in the Holy Land to upheaval. They do not draw a border to fight for.
